Research topic

Compact instrumentation with rotated chirped Bragg gratings

Most tabletop optical instruments that work with the spectrum of light, such as spectrometers and pulse shapers, follow the same recipe: a diffraction grating spreads the wavelengths out in angle, a lens or mirror turns that angular spread into a spatial one, and some free-space distance is needed for the colors to separate. The free-space propagation and the collimating optics set the size of the instrument, and they need careful alignment.

A chirped volume Bragg grating is a block of glass in which the period of a recorded refractive-index grating changes along its length, so that each wavelength is reflected at a different depth. Together with colleagues at CREOL, University of Central Florida, we rotated this grating by 45° with respect to the faces of the block. Each wavelength is then reflected sideways at a different position and leaves through a side face, so the spectrum of a normally incident beam is spread out in space inside the glass, with no free-space propagation and no collimating lens. Rotation also removes the link between the length and the width of the device: in a conventional chirped Bragg grating both must grow to cover a wider bandwidth.

We have used these rotated chirped Bragg gratings (r-CBGs) as compact spectrometers and as the basis for space-time wave packet synthesizers, where a pair of r-CBGs with a phase plate between them replaces a tabletop arrangement of diffraction gratings and lenses. To develop r-CBG spectrometers beyond the laboratory, I co-founded Tau Optics, a CREOL spin-out, which received an NSF STTR Phase I award to build a prototype of a compact, high-precision spectrometer based on r-CBGs.

Key results

  • Rotated chirped volume Bragg gratings. A chirped Bragg grating rotated by 45° inside a glass block spatially resolves the spectrum of a normally incident beam in a compact footprint, without free-space propagation or collimation. We fabricated and characterized devices in several spectral windows, and showed that a cascaded pair of r-CBGs first resolves the spectrum and then recombines it into a single beam. Opt. Lett. 48, 1180 (2023)
  • Space-time wave packets from a centimeter-scale device. Placing a phase plate between two cascaded r-CBGs imposes the spatial-frequency–wavelength correlation that defines a space-time wave packet. The whole synthesizer occupies 25 × 25 × 8 mm3, orders of magnitude smaller than the free-space arrangements used before, and it avoids the precise alignment that those free-space systems require. Opt. Lett. 48, 2500 (2023)
  • Two spectral bands in one volume. Two r-CBGs, rotated by +45° and −45°, are recorded in the same glass block. Each one spreads out the spectrum of its own band and sends it through a different face, so a single device analyzes two contiguous or separated spectral windows in the visible and near-infrared without becoming larger. Opt. Lett. 48, 5137 (2023)
  • Patents. The r-CBG spectrometer and the use of a pair of rotated volume Bragg gratings for spectral phase modulation are covered by two U.S. patents, listed below. U.S. Patents 12,613,365 and 12,625,067
